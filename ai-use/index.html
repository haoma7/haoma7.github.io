AI Use in Coursework, Research, and Communication

These expectations apply to students taking my courses and those working with me on research. AI can be useful, but the work you share should reflect your own understanding. Follow the AI-use requirements for your assignment or research project, be transparent about how you use it, and take responsibility for everything you submit. When disclosing AI use, explain what you used it for and how you checked the resulting work.

Communicate clearly

Emails, assignments, research updates, and papers are all forms of communication. In each case, you’re helping someone understand your ideas, reasoning, or findings. Your reader shouldn’t need to be Shakespeare to make sense of your email or report. Make your main point easy to follow, use plain language, and include the details needed to support your reasoning. Remove anything that adds length without adding meaning.

For emails to me, simple and direct is best. I’d rather read a short message in your own voice than a long, AI-polished email full of elaborate wording. You don’t need perfect English or overly formal language—just tell me what you want to say or ask. If you use AI to help with your writing, make sure the final message reflects your meaning and uses words you understand and would feel comfortable using in a conversation.

Clear communication also means being honest about where you are in your work. Tell me what you understand, what you have tried, and where you are stuck. That gives us a useful starting point for discussion.

Understand and take responsibility for your work

You are responsible for the accuracy and quality of anything you submit or share, including material produced with AI assistance. Check the claims, references, code, and results. If you don’t understand something well enough to evaluate it, work through it or ask for help before presenting it as completed work.

Be prepared to explain your code and reasoning, justify your decisions, and apply the concepts independently. In research, this also means understanding your own contribution, the evidence supporting your findings, and the limitations of your work.

I may require code walkthroughs for programming assignments, similar to technical interviews, and ask questions during project presentations. I may also arrange follow-up, in-person discussions when I need to better understand your contribution or assess your understanding.

If you submit 100 pages, you must be prepared to discuss any part of those 100 pages, whether or not you disclose AI use.

Build and maintain trust

Working together requires trust. This is especially important in a research project or thesis, where your supervisor needs to know what you understand, what you contributed, and what you can do independently. Being open about uncertainty and asking questions helps build that trust. I don’t expect you to know everything.

Trust develops gradually through consistent, reliable work. Repeatedly presenting work you cannot explain, or giving a misleading impression of your own contribution, can damage that trust. Rebuilding that trust takes time and consistent changes in how you work and communicate.

Disclosing AI use and receiving a good assignment grade do not, by themselves, resolve concerns about your understanding or independence. Disclosure tells me how the work was produced; our discussions and your ability to work through problems help me assess what you have learned.

To confidently rely on your research contributions, recommend you for opportunities, or write a strong reference letter, I need to see evidence of your understanding, judgment, and independent work over time.

Build habits that support independent thinking

The habits you develop now matter beyond a single assignment. Take time to sit with a problem, think through possible approaches, and make an attempt before turning to AI for an immediate answer. When you do use AI, question its suggestions and check whether you can explain and apply what you have learned.

Each time you work through a difficulty, you practise the thinking skills you will need later. If you repeatedly hand that thinking over to AI, you risk becoming dependent on it. Habits become harder to change once they are established, which is why I raise this concern early.

That dependence can become a serious obstacle when you reach a research project or thesis and need to make independent progress toward graduation. You will also encounter problems where AI gives unreliable answers, cannot help, or is unavailable. You need practice judging whether an answer makes sense and working through unfamiliar problems yourself.

Focus on learning and meaningful progress

AI can help you produce more text, code, or paper drafts in less time. But the number of pages or drafts you produce is not, by itself, a measure of learning or research progress. Take time to understand what you’re doing, question the results, and consider what the work contributes.

Before sharing your work, ask yourself:

  • Can I explain the ideas, code, and decisions in my own words?
  • Have I checked the accuracy of the claims, references, and results?
  • Can I apply what I have learned to a similar problem independently?
  • What is my own contribution, and why does it matter?
  • What remains uncertain, and where do I need help?

My goal, both in teaching and supervision, is to help you become an independent thinker and researcher. Your ability to ask good questions, evaluate evidence, and work through difficult problems will matter well beyond the grade you receive in a course. Use AI in ways that help you develop those abilities.
