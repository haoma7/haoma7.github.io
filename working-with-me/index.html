Working With Me

If you are taking a course with me or considering working with me, here is what to expect: how I communicate, what I value, and what I ask of the people I work with.

How I work

I value direct communication, a willingness to question ideas, and taking responsibility for our work. I welcome disagreement and recognise that I can be wrong. If you think I’ve made a mistake, disagree with my approach, or have concerns about how we are working together, please tell me. You don’t need to agree with me just because I’m your instructor or supervisor. I aim to give clear, honest feedback. If my reasoning does not make sense to you, ask me to explain it.

Communicate clearly

Emails, assignments, research updates, and papers are all forms of communication. In each case, you’re helping someone understand your ideas, reasoning, or findings. Your reader shouldn’t need to be Shakespeare to make sense of your email or report. Make your main point easy to follow, use plain language, and include the details needed to support your reasoning. Remove anything that adds length without adding meaning.

For emails to me, simple and direct is best. I’d rather read a short message in your own voice than a long, AI-polished email full of elaborate wording. You don’t need perfect English or overly formal language—just tell me what you want to say or ask. If you use AI to help with your writing, make sure the final message reflects your meaning and uses words you understand and would feel comfortable using in a conversation.

Clear communication also means being honest about where you are in your work. Tell me what you understand, what you have tried, and where you are stuck. That gives us a useful starting point for discussion.

Replying to emails

I try to answer straightforward questions as soon as I can. If you are a student and have not heard back about a straightforward question within two working days, excluding weekends and holidays, send me a brief follow-up. Reviewing a document or evaluating your work takes more thought, so those replies may take longer.

When I send you a question or propose a next step, please reply. If you ask me for help and I spend time finding a solution, let me know whether it works for you. If your plans have changed or you no longer need the help, say so. A brief reply lets me know whether the issue is resolved and acknowledges the time spent trying to help.

Build and maintain trust

Working together requires trust. This is especially important in a research project or thesis, where your collaborators—including your teammates and supervisor—need to know what you understand, what you contributed, and what you can do independently. Being open about uncertainty and asking questions helps build that trust. I don’t expect you to know everything.

Trust develops gradually through consistent, reliable work. Repeatedly presenting work you cannot explain, or giving a misleading impression of your own contribution, can damage that trust. Rebuilding that trust takes time and consistent changes in how you work and communicate.

For students I supervise, my confidence in recommending you for opportunities or writing a strong reference letter comes from seeing your understanding, judgment, and independent work over time.

Understand and take responsibility for your work

You are responsible for the accuracy and quality of anything you submit or share, including material produced with AI assistance. Check the claims, references, code, and results. If you don’t understand something well enough to evaluate it, work through it or ask for help before presenting it as completed work.

Be prepared to explain your code, justify your decisions, and discuss the evidence and limitations behind your findings.

These expectations go both ways: I should also be able to explain my reasoning, acknowledge uncertainty, and take responsibility for my contributions.

For coursework and student projects

I may require walkthroughs for assignments, similar to technical interviews, and ask questions during project presentations. I may also arrange follow-up discussions when I need to better understand your contribution or assess your understanding.

If you submit 100 pages, you must be prepared to discuss any part of those 100 pages, whether or not you disclose AI use.

Code is cheap. Show me the talk.

Use AI thoughtfully and think independently

Be transparent about your use of AI: explain what you used it for and how you checked the resulting work.

For students

Make an attempt before turning to AI for an immediate answer. Question its suggestions and check whether you can apply what you have learned independently. You will need that practice when AI gives unreliable answers or cannot help.

Disclosing AI use explains how the work was produced; it does not, by itself, demonstrate understanding. Beyond assignment grades, I also look at how you explain your reasoning and apply what you have learned independently.

A research project or thesis requires independent progress. Use AI in ways that prepare you for that work.

Focus on learning and meaningful progress

Meaning matters to me. I want us to do work that is technically exciting, helps someone, or contributes something useful to society. Building something cool counts. So does solving an everyday problem that makes someone’s life a little easier. We spend a real part of our lives on this work, and I want it to be worth that time.

That is why I care about what we learn and what our work contributes. The number of pages or drafts we produce is not, by itself, a measure of progress. Take time to understand what you’re doing, question the results, and ask why the work matters.

Before sharing your work, ask yourself:

  • Can I explain the ideas, code, and decisions in my own words?
  • Have I checked the accuracy of the claims, references, and results?
  • Can I apply what I have learned to a similar problem independently?
  • What is my own contribution, and why does it matter?
  • What remains uncertain, and where do I need help?
